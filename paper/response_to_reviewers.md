# Response to the Reviewers: Paper 177

**GNN-AdaptGraph: Adaptive Graph Runtime Optimization Using GraphSAGE and Communication-Aware Repartitioning**

We thank both reviewers for their careful and constructive comments. Following them up showed
that several results in the submitted version did not support its claims:

- the reported speedups were measurement noise (the executor ignored the controller's actions);
- the controller imitated a hand-written rule;
- ACGR's "boundary communication" term duplicated the edge-cut term.

We therefore rebuilt the system and the evaluation, and rewrote the manuscript. Every number in
the revised paper is generated by released scripts. No number from the original Tables 2–3 or
Figs. 3–5 is reused.

The revised results are more nuanced than our original claims, and we report them as they are:

- **Thread adjustment works.** It is now implemented and measured, and gives up to 1.25× over
  static partitioning.
- **ACGR is a strong local repairer.** On sparse graphs it beats label-propagation refinement.
- **The learned controller makes better per-decision choices than a tuned rule.** End to end,
  however, it performs on par with simple heuristics.
- **Periodic global repartitioning wins under steady drift.** It does so on the real temporal
  stream, and the controller does not learn this.

Below, each comment is quoted and followed by our response, with the location in the revision
(section, equation, table and figure numbers refer to the revised PDF).

---

## Reviewer #2

**R2.1** *Please clarify the actual role of GraphSAGE in runtime decision-making… training
procedure, input representations, target labels or reward-generation mechanism, training
dataset, and learned parameters are not sufficiently specified.*

The submitted controller was trained on labels from a fixed threshold rule, so it could not
learn anything beyond that rule. In the revision:

- **Input (Sect. 4.1).** The communication graph of the current partitioning: one node per
  partition, 8 scale-free partition features and 3 global runtime features.
- **Model (Sect. 4.2).** 2 GraphSAGE layers with the mean aggregator, hidden dimension 64, and a
  mean‖max readout (14,244 parameters). An MLP ablation removes message passing.
- **Labels (Sect. 4.3, Eq. 5).** Labels are outcome-based. A look-ahead oracle evaluates all
  four actions (NoAction, ThreadAdjust, Rebalance via ACGR, Repartition via METIS) over 8 future
  windows, using a cost model calibrated on the real executor.
- **Loss (Eq. 7).** Training minimises the expected regret.

**R2.2** *Kindly provide a rigorous comparison with established graph partitioning and
repartitioning methods… METIS/ParMETIS, streaming partitioning, or other communication-aware
approaches.*

- **E1, Table 2.** Static partitioning compares METIS, recursive Kernighan–Lin, LDG, FENNEL and
  random hashing. Each is combined with no refinement, label-propagation refinement (as in
  Spinner), ACGR, and a cut-only ACGR ablation, on four graphs, with k ∈ {4, 8, 16} and 5 seeds.
- **E3, Table 4.** The dynamic comparison covers Static, Always-ThreadAdjust, Always-ACGR,
  Periodic METIS scratch-remap (the scheme behind ParMETIS-style adaptive repartitioning), a
  tuned threshold rule, model-predictive control (MPC) with the same cost model, the MLP
  ablation, and the look-ahead oracle as an upper bound.

**R2.3** *Please strengthen the experimental validation of ACGR. On Cora, ACGR reduces the
edge cut from 744 to 743…*

We found two causes and fixed both (Sect. 4.4):

1. The "boundary communication" term always equals twice the edge cut. It is replaced by true
   communication volume (Eq. 2).
2. All imbalance was penalised, so every move away from the balanced Kernighan–Lin start looked
   worse. Imbalance within a tolerance ε is now an explicit constraint (Eq. 9).

Moves are evaluated incrementally in O(deg v), and tests check the incremental computation
against a full recomputation. Results (Sect. 6.2, Table 2, k=8):

- From degraded (streaming) partitions, ACGR reduces the edge cut by 41% on PubMed and 37% on
  Cora, against 32% and 26% for label propagation.
- It gives the lowest communication volume of all refiners on the sparse graphs.
- From METIS starts, it lowers communication volume by about 4%.

We also report its limits. METIS from scratch remains better. On the dense power-law
sx-mathoverflow graph, local refinement gains little.

**R2.4** *Kindly address the inconsistency between the claimed dynamic/adaptive framework
and the datasets used… Experiments involving actual graph mutations, streaming updates, or
time-varying workloads are required.*

All adaptation experiments now run on dynamic graphs (Sect. 5, Table 1):

- **Real stream:** SNAP sx-mathoverflow, with 390,441 timestamped interactions over 6.4 years in
  30 windows. Edges expire, so the graph both grows and shrinks.
- **Synthetic streams:** PubMed streams with community drift, activity hotspots and vertex
  growth.

Only the vertices touched in a window and their neighbours are recomputed, so the workload
varies over time.

**R2.5** *Please report complete details of the GraphSAGE training and evaluation protocol…*

Sect. 5 ("Controller training") reports the full protocol:

- **Data:** 7,200 states from 450 synthetic streams.
- **Optimisation:** AdamW with learning rate 1e-3 and weight decay 1e-4, batch size 64,
  ReduceLROnPlateau, early stopping on validation regret (at most 300 epochs), and 3 seeds.
- **Split:** 70/15/15 by stream. The real evaluation stream is never used in training.
- **Results:** the label distribution is in Sect. 6.3, and training traces and the confusion
  matrix are in Fig. 2.

We also disclose a model iteration. Trained only on our first synthetic stream family, the
controller never learned to repartition. We then added a second family of synthetic
interaction streams, motivated by general properties of temporal networks and not fitted to
the test stream. The first-iteration results are kept in the released artefact.

**R2.6** *Kindly clarify the reward formulation and sign conventions. Equation (16)…
normalization and selection of the weights w1–w4.*

Eq. (16) is removed. The objective is now the end-to-end time of the stream (Eq. 3), the sum of:

- execution time;
- emulated network time;
- adaptation run time;
- migration time;
- controller time.

All terms are in seconds and all are minimised, so no weights w1–w4 are needed. In ACGR's
surrogate objective (Eq. 8), every term is normalised to [0, 1] and is a cost.

**R2.7** *Please provide repeated experimental runs and statistical analysis…*

Measurement (Sect. 5, "Protocol and statistics"):

- Every configuration is executed 10 times on the real executor, with policies interleaved in
  random order.
- We report the per-window median. Outlier runs caused by OS paging are counted and reported.
- Totals come with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, and synthetic streams use 3 seeds.
- Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests against Static are marked in Table 4.
- Controllers are trained with 3 seeds, and standard deviations are reported (Table 3).

**R2 overall** *…lack of genuine dynamic-graph experiments; insufficient description and
validation of the learned controller; absence of established partitioning baselines;
extremely small ACGR improvement; limited scalability evaluation; missing statistical
analysis; unclear separation of adaptation overhead from execution gains; the negative result
on PubMed…*

All points are addressed above. In addition:

- **Overheads** are measured and charged separately (Eq. 3): GraphSAGE's controller time is
  2.6% of the total. MPC's speculative repartitioning makes it slower than Static in every
  scenario.
- **Scale** covers k ∈ {4, 8, 16} (static) and {4, 8} (dynamic), graphs of 2.7k–25k vertices,
  and three platforms (shared memory, emulated 10 GbE and 1 GbE).
- **The original PubMed result** was an artefact. The original executor ignored actions, and the
  controller had not selected repartitioning there.

The revised evaluation answers the question honestly:

- Adaptation pays, by up to 1.25× over Static.
- GraphSAGE has the lowest decision regret (0.69% vs 0.91% for a tuned rule; Table 3).
- End to end, it stays within 4.3% of the best deployable policy on average, but does not beat
  simple heuristics.
- On the real stream, periodic METIS is best (up to 1.13× vs the oracle's 1.16×).

Sect. 6.5 discusses when each adaptation pays off.

**Others:** *Figures can be improved. Please adhere Springer template. Please improve the
reference format too. Recent articles reviews can be included.*

- **Figures:** redrawn as vector graphics. The two architecture figures are merged into one
  (Fig. 1), and the radar chart is removed.
- **Template:** the paper uses the official LNCS class (llncs.cls v2.26) with splncs04.bst and
  is 15 pages.
- **References:** every reference is generated from its Crossref record. This corrected three
  entries in the original list: a wrong first author for "An exact algorithm for the minimum
  gap graph partitioning problem", and a wrong volume and wrong pages in two others.
- **Recent work:** we added 2023–2026 work on dynamic graph processing, dynamic and streaming
  partitioning, and distributed GNN systems, together with the classical baselines.

See the response to R2 "Others".

**R5.3** *Result analysis parts should be more elaborate… a clear recommendation of the paper
is missing.*

Sect. 6 analyses each experiment separately: executor, partition quality, controller and
end-to-end. Sect. 6.5 gives explicit recommendations:

- use thread reallocation for transient hotspots;
- use global repartitioning under persistent drift;
- never repartition speculatively or unconditionally.

A cheap thread-adjustment trigger combined with drift-triggered global repartitioning is the
practical choice. Sect. 6.5 also states the limitations.

**R5.4** *The paper should be properly checked for typos and grammatical mistakes… better
proof read.*

The manuscript was rewritten and proofread throughout.

**R5.5** *There is a lot of scope to enrich and improve the standards of topic.*

The revision adds:
- a real temporal dataset;
- an implemented and measured executor;
- outcome-based learning;
- established baselines, including MPC;
- statistical testing;
- a reproducible artefact (code, seeds and scripts for every table and figure).
